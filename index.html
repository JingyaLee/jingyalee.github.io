02 — Research

Why People Choose the Places They Do

My research asked a deceptively simple question: when given options, how and why do people gravitate toward certain spaces? The answers revealed how deeply context — physical, social, and situational — shapes spatial preference.

01

The Two-Park Study

Surveying users across two adjacent parks — one immediately beside a train station, one five minutes further — I found that physical proximity to transit fundamentally changes the nature of a visit. The station-side park attracted largely incidental visitors: people pausing on their way elsewhere. The quieter park drew intentional visitors — dog walkers, regulars, people there specifically for the park itself.

"The same type of space, 400 metres apart, serves entirely different human needs."

Spatial proximity to transit shapes the purpose — and nature — of a visit

02

Choosing Places with Others

Using the Evaluation Grid Method (EGM), I mapped how people's criteria for choosing public places — cafés, parks — shift depending on who they are with. The same person applies different spatial standards when alone, with a partner, with friends, or with family. Social context is a hidden variable in spatial preference.

"Open and exposed with friends. Enclosed and quiet with a partner. Spatial need is social need."

EGM revealed how social context reshapes spatial preference criteria

03 — Practice

Computation in Architectural Work

In my current role at an architectural consultancy, I work at the intersection of BIM and process automation — developing systems that reduce human error and improve workflow efficiency. One project involved contributing to an algorithm that automatically generates office layout proposals based on occupant movement patterns.

This experience surfaced the central tension I bring to the UCL programme: computational systems can process behavioral data, but they rarely ask what that data means to the people it describes. Optimisation and human experience are not the same thing.

Data-driven office layout: occupant movement patterns informing spatial arrangement

04 — Why UCL

Making Human Experience Computable

The MSc Architectural Computation at UCL is the specific context I need — a programme that treats computation not as a technical layer but as a design medium and a mode of thinking. I want to develop the tools to translate what I know about human spatial experience into frameworks that can inform computational design.

Interdisciplinary Framing

The programme's integration of architecture, engineering, and computer science maps directly onto my own trajectory — I have lived across these disciplines and want to synthesise them.

Computation as Reflection

I am drawn to the programme's critical stance — its insistence on questioning how computational methods shape thinking, not just what they produce.

Research-Led Studio

Studio and research modules that treat design as a knowledge process align with my background in empirical research and my interest in connecting evidence to built form.
